\abstract{We ask whether depreciation of a hypothetical local-currency debt reduces a borrower's USD-valued repayment after financing and transaction costs, and how the answer changes with observed loan duration, sample selection and available FX marks. We use 130,742 MakerDAO ETH-A debt draws only as recorded size and timing inputs for Argentine-peso (ARS) and Turkish-lira (TRY) counterfactuals. The 7,105 selected clean, repaid spells last a median 6.95 days; at published daily reference rates and an illustrative 20\% effective annual borrowing rate, their median net benefits are $-2.86$\% (ARS) and $-4.30$\% (TRY). Twelve-month medians of 2.67\% and 6.59\% instead map all eligible draws to imposed holding periods. On the \emph{same clean-spell starts} held for 12 months, the medians are 0.56\% and $-4.70$\%, showing that the contrast reflects sample composition as well as horizon and FX frequency. Previous-publication-date marks, borrowing and transaction-cost grids, urn-level summaries and concentration checks bound the interpretation. Official FX references are not demonstrated executable prices, especially for ARS; liquidation frequency and issuer solvency are not estimated. The contribution is a reproducible separation of these distinct empirical and accounting objects, rather than a new FX mechanism or evidence of a deployed local-currency protocol.}

\section{Introduction}
Local-currency borrowing in decentralised finance is worth examining because the denomination of a debt determines who bears exchange-rate changes. A borrower retaining USD-valued proceeds but owing a fixed number of local-currency units (LCU) may see the USD value of that liability decline after depreciation; the issuer or claimholder must still finance and redeem the LCU claim. This is a potential redistribution of FX exposure, not a measurement of consumer-price inflation or a free gain. It is uncertain whether a usable LCU token, accessible conversion market, sustainable lender price or sufficiently long collateralised loan would make this accounting scenario feasible.

Our research question is: \emph{given recorded on-chain draw sizes, dates and selected repayment durations, what is the conditional USD cash-flow effect of a stipulated ARS- or TRY-denominated liability, and how sensitive is it to duration, sample composition, published FX timing and costs?} The object is the voluntary-repayment counterfactual for a borrower holding the hypothetical proceeds in USD. A MakerDAO ETH-A draw is actually DAI-denominated, so none of the mapped local-currency loans or conversions occurred in the dataset.

The contribution is a measurement design that separates three objects often conflated in a headline result: short selected lifecycles versus imposed holding periods; event, urn and principal-weighted summaries; and published reference FX versus an executable quote. We decompose the hypothetical cash flow into FX movement, interest and execution costs, compare the same clean-spell starts under different horizons, and expose sensitivity to costs and a purely illustrative official-to-accessible FX wedge. A conditional collateral-sale ledger prevents charging a liquidated borrower for a later voluntary repayment, while the available data justify no probability-weighted liquidation return. These checks do not constitute a new exchange-rate theory or an empirical evaluation of an operating LCU protocol.

The paper first situates the question in currency-mismatch and DeFi research, then presents a unified data and methodology section. Results distinguish what changes when the holding period, sample, FX mark or unit of observation changes. The discussion addresses borrower interpretation, issuer accounting and the limits of extrapolating from MakerDAO to an LCU market.

\section{Related research and research gap}
\subsection{Debt denomination and exchange-rate exposure}
Work on currency mismatches and ``original sin'' shows why the denomination of debt matters for emerging-market balance sheets. Eichengreen, Hausmann and Panizza separate the consequences of currency mismatch from proposed explanations for why some borrowers cannot obtain domestic-currency funding \cite{EichengreenHausmannPanizza2003}. In the conventional case of a borrower with local-currency income and foreign-currency debt, depreciation can increase the local-currency burden; the balance-sheet channel is central to the model of C\'espedes, Chang and Velasco \cite{CespedesChangVelasco2004}. Our assumed exposure points in the other direction: proceeds are retained at USD value and the liability is fixed in ARS or TRY. The sign of the conversion term reverses for that particular borrower, while the counterparty and access costs cannot be ignored. The paper does not infer a macroeconomic benefit from the borrower's nominal debt erosion.

Salomao and Varela model firms' choice of currency denomination jointly with investment and show empirically that characteristics of firms borrowing in foreign currency matter for aggregate conclusions \cite{SalomaoVarela2022}. That selection result is directly relevant to our use of MakerDAO: the observed sizes and entry dates of DAI draws cannot stand in for the demand schedule of peso or lira borrowers. Our clean-spell restriction selects repaid, reconstructible positions as well. The sample-composition and same-start comparisons therefore serve as checks on the interpretation, rather than estimates of who would actually take an LCU loan.

\subsection{On-chain lending, liquidation and stablecoins}
Sch\"ar and Werner et al. describe the transparent transaction logic and composability of DeFi lending, while also stressing collateral, oracle and execution constraints \cite{Schar2021,Werner2022}. Chaleenutthawut et al. make a MakerDAO debt-trace archive available for loan-portfolio analysis \cite{Chaleenutthawut2024}; it provides timestamps and debt changes, not observations of hypothetical ARS or TRY contracts. The trace extraction omits top-level calls, which is relevant to identifying complete spells. Qin et al. empirically study liquidations across major lending protocols and show that auctions and discounted collateral sales affect borrowers \cite{Qin2021Liquidations}. Their measurements cannot supply a liquidation probability or sale path for the LCU liabilities stipulated here. We therefore use the collateral-sale identity only to define a separate close-out branch.

Research on stablecoin prices examines redemption, arbitrage and collateral dynamics at a system level \cite{Lyons2023,KlagesMundt2022,GortonZhang2023}. A reduced USD mark on one hypothetical borrower's debt says nothing by itself about an issuer's reserve adequacy, the token's peg or redemption performance. The earlier literatures establish the economic mechanism and the on-chain context, but do not answer our narrower question: how the apparent borrower benefit changes when a MakerDAO-based LCU counterfactual respects observed short durations, the selected sample, publication-date FX marks and the concentration of debt draws. That combination of reproducible checks is the contribution of this article; actual adoption, executable pricing and protocol solvency require different data.

\section{Data and methodology}
\subsection{Draw events, urns, and clean spells}
The public replication archive accompanying Chaleenutthawut et al. \cite{Chaleenutthawut2024} contains 4,466,880 successful MakerDAO Vat traces. Using the documented debt-change calculation for \texttt{frob} \cite{MakerVatDocs}, we retain 130,742 positive ETH-A draws of at least 1 DAI between January 2020 and July 2023, totalling 13.318 billion DAI, in 16,846 urn identifiers (Table~\ref{tab:construction}). A draw is an event, not an independent borrower or necessarily a complete lifecycle; an urn is a vault identifier, not a verified person. The source extraction excludes top-level calls. If an omitted call changes debt between two retained traces, an apparent single draw and repayment may be misclassified as a complete spell, or its reconstructed opening and closing dates may be wrong. We cannot measure the frequency or direction of this error without the missing call records. The source query, decoder, selection rules and archive checksum are in the repository.

The resulting debt-change distribution is highly skewed: the median is 4,792 DAI, the mean 101,865 DAI and the maximum 150.45 million DAI. These values are independently recoverable from the tracked processed event file. The transaction hash and urn remain attached to each event for auditing. A positive \texttt{dart} can represent a further draw from an existing vault; neither the number of rows nor the number of urns identifies the number of people. In particular, a draw does not establish the time at which its associated debt was ultimately repaid.

MakerDAO is useful here because ETH-A debt draws provide recorded principal changes, UTC event times and urn identifiers under a transparent on-chain collateralised-debt mechanism. We map a DAI draw's USD-equivalent size and calendar exposure to a \emph{stipulated} LCU liability, then vary the hypothetical closing date; this holds the size and timing inputs fixed while exposing the effect of an FX path and costs. It does not follow that ARS or TRY borrowers would choose those loan sizes or entry months, face the same collateral constraints, or have access to equivalent liquidity. These data have one asset type and one historical adoption window, repeated activity within urns, skewed sizes and selected reconstructible closures. Thus neither the event distribution nor the clean-spell duration distribution represents an LCU credit market. We report size floors, urn summaries and month concentration to make these constraints visible, not to claim external validity.

We reconstruct a restricted subset of single-draw, single-repayment, non-forked clean spells. Of 7,947 candidate clean repaid spells, 136 have non-positive reconstructed durations and 706 of the remaining 7,811 started before the analysis window. The 7,105 selected spells have both monthly and daily FX coverage for each currency; no further spell is lost for lack of a published FX endpoint. Their median observed duration is 6.95 days. These are selected repaid spells, and no duration is observed for the population of all draw events. Their timestamps determine an observed \emph{spell length}, while ARS/TRY cash flows remain \emph{counterfactual}; MakerDAO issued no LCU loan.

\subsection{Published exchange-rate references}
$E_t$ denotes LCU per USD. The fixed-horizon scenarios retain archived OECD monthly-average series \texttt{ARGCCUSMA02STM} and \texttt{CCUSMA02TRM618N} distributed through FRED \cite{FREDARS,FREDTRY}. The archived FX paths extend beyond the July 2023 draw window, so a 24-month endpoint remains covered; Figure~\ref{fig:fx} shows their marked depreciation and the different timing of ARS and TRY movements. Using a monthly series for a seven-day spell is problematic: entry and exit in one calendar month share one rate and gross FX benefit becomes exactly zero. For the observed-duration analysis we use archived daily published references: the BCRA Communication A3500 wholesale ARS/USD reference \cite{BCRADaily} and the ECB's TRY/EUR rate divided by its USD/EUR rate to obtain TRY/USD \cite{ECBDaily}. For each UTC event date the code takes the most recent published reference \emph{calendar date no later than that date}, without interpolation or a future date. On nonpublication days it carries forward the preceding quote. The median quote-date lag is zero, its 95th percentile two days and its maximum four days across the selected endpoints. The same spells are also valued at monthly means from their daily source and at the original OECD/FRED monthly marks, distinguishing frequency from source.

These marks are retrospective reference valuations; a published quote on the same date could have appeared after the on-chain event. We therefore rerun the selected-spell result using the \emph{strictly previous published calendar date} at both endpoints. This is a timing sensitivity, not evidence that that earlier reference was executable. BCRA's wholesale reference is not a demonstrated retail conversion price. An Argentine rate accessible under exchange controls can differ materially from any official series. No independently vetted parallel-market series is included, so daily frequency fixes the zero-within-month artefact but cannot resolve market access or intraday execution.

If an accessible ARS rate were $E_t^{\rm access}=w_t E_t^{\rm official}$ for a wedge $w_t>0$, the gross effect would be $1-(w_0/w_h)(E_0^{\rm official}/E_h^{\rm official})$. A constant wedge cancels under this symmetric entry/exit convention, but a changing ratio $w_h/w_0$ can reverse the sign. We compute several \emph{stipulated relative wedges} below without pretending to observe a parallel-rate path.

\subsection{Conditional borrower cash flows}
\subsubsection{Voluntary repayment}
For a draw of $P_i$ USD-equivalent DAI at origination, hypothetical initial LCU debt is $L_{i,0}=P_iE_{i,0}$. With an \emph{effective annual} LCU borrowing rate $r$, and no intermediate payments, its USD value at voluntary closing after $t$ years is
\begin{equation}
D_i(t)=P_i\frac{E_{i,0}}{E_{i,t}}(1+r)^t,\qquad
G_i(t)=P_i-D_i(t)\big|_{r=0}.
\label{eq:debt}
\end{equation}
The protocol fee $fP_i$ is paid externally in USD at opening and is \emph{not financed}. We assume a conversion friction $sP_i$ on the opening swap of hypothetical LCU proceeds into USD and $sD_i(t)$ on acquiring LCU for voluntary repayment. The illustrative gas budget $g$ is split equally: $g/2$ at opening and $g/2$ for voluntary closing. The incremental USD cash-accounting benefit relative to holding the same collateral without borrowing is
\begin{equation}
B_i^{\rm repay}(t)=P_i-fP_i-sP_i-\tfrac{g}{2}
-D_i(t)-sD_i(t)-\tfrac{g}{2}.
\label{eq:repay}
\end{equation}
We retain the hypothetical proceeds at USD par with no investment return to isolate the currency denomination of the liability from an additional risky asset return. This is a clearly specified exposure case, not an assertion about how actual LCU borrowers spend proceeds; someone consuming in local currency or investing in a volatile asset would have a different USD comparison. The original collateral returns on repayment and cancels against the same collateral held without borrowing. Thus $B$ is an incremental cash-accounting difference, not investor profit or welfare.

Actual LCU conversions, market spread, fee funding, interest payments and taxes are unobserved. Baseline assumptions are $r=20\%$, $f=0.5\%$, $s=0.3\%$ per conversion and $g=\$40$. The round 20\% rate is an illustrative financing-cost benchmark, not a historical MakerDAO stability fee or an observed ARS/TRY loan offer. Likewise, the fee, spread and gas numbers are explicit modelling choices rather than measured prices for a deployed LCU protocol. Table~\ref{tab:assumptions} specifies each timing convention; rate, conversion and gas grids test whether the findings rely on these choices. None of the grid endpoints is claimed to represent a verified market quote.

The fee timing matters: charging the opening fee on discounted debt at closure would make its dollar value depend on the later FX rate and yield a different net series. We use an externally paid opening fee throughout. USD 40 covers only the two specified borrower transactions, not auction gas, oracle operation or all protocol transactions. Conversion friction in the liquidation branch is charged at entry only, because that stylised borrower makes no voluntary closing swap.

\subsubsection{Mutually exclusive collateral sale}
If the collateral is sold at time $\tau$ rather than voluntarily repaid, its USD mark before sale is $V_{i,\tau}$. A proportional auction haircut $a$ gives $S_{i,\tau}=(1-a)V_{i,\tau}$. Under an explicit nonrecourse accounting convention, recovered debt is $R_{i,\tau}=\min(S_{i,\tau},D_i(\tau))$, and a penalty $\lambda D_i(\tau)$ is collected \emph{only from surplus}:
\begin{align}
\Pi_{i,\tau}&=\min\{\lambda D_i(\tau),[S_{i,\tau}-D_i(\tau)]_+\},\\
H_{i,\tau}&=[S_{i,\tau}-D_i(\tau)]_+-\Pi_{i,\tau},&
W_{i,\tau}&=[D_i(\tau)-S_{i,\tau}]_+,\\
B_i^{\rm liq}(\tau)&=P_i-fP_i-sP_i-\tfrac{g}{2}
-V_{i,\tau}+H_{i,\tau}.
\label{eq:liquidation}
\end{align}
$H$ is cash returned to the borrower; $W$ is the unrecovered loan claim. $V-H$ is the collateral value lost relative to holding it without borrowing. The borrower neither pays $D_i(\tau)$ again in cash nor repays $D_i(h)$ at the original horizon $h$. There is no voluntary exit swap or closing gas in this branch. Nonrecourse is an explicit \emph{hypothetical close-out convention}, consistent with restricting recovery to posted collateral in this particular ledger; no legal recourse rule for a proposed LCU platform is inferred. If other assets or an enforceable personal claim could cover a shortfall, the allocation of $W$ would differ.

The purpose of this branch is to prevent a conceptual double charge when interpreting possible borrower outcomes. The detailed numerical surplus and shortfall checks are in the accompanying model note and executable tests. Available collateral shocks do not reveal $\tau$, oracle state, auction proceeds or haircut. Equation~\eqref{eq:liquidation} is an implemented \emph{conditional ledger}, not an estimated branch frequency or expected benefit. A deterministic terminal-ratio diagnostic is kept in the Appendix and is never used to modify the voluntary-repayment medians.

\subsection{Empirical comparisons and robustness}
We distinguish three counterfactual mappings. First, 7,105 selected clean spells use their observed start and repayment timestamps, $t=\text{days}/365.25$ and published daily FX dates; original OECD/FRED and matched-source monthly values serve as frequency checks. Second, exactly those \emph{same 7,105 starting positions} are each assigned 3-, 6-, 12- or 24-month OECD/FRED endpoints. Third, all 130,742 eligible draw events receive the same fixed-horizon assignments. Three and six months show intermediate horizons around an annual comparison; 12 and 24 months illustrate longer exposure to depreciation. These lengths are round scenario choices, not estimated MakerDAO tenors. Comparing the second and third mappings at 12 months fixes the horizon and FX source but changes the selected sample. Comparing observed and fixed mappings on the same starts holds sample membership fixed but changes both the horizon and the part of the historical FX path observed; it is not a causal duration experiment. Table~\ref{tab:samesample} reports these contrasts.

We report event medians, positive shares and $\sum_i B_i/\sum_iP_i$; the last is a \emph{signed amount-weighted ratio}, not an average borrower's return. A decomposition of the weighted numerator into gross FX, interest, fee, conversion and gas terms reconciles exactly with this ratio. Component medians are not added as though they were the median of a sum. Means remain machine-readable but can be extremely negative for tiny draws facing fixed gas.

For dependence, we tabulate draws per urn, the chronologically first eligible draw from each urn, and an urn aggregate $100\sum_{i\in u}B_i/\sum_{i\in u}P_i$. The pooled amount-weighted result is algebraically identical for all draws and urn aggregates; this is not evidence of independence. First-draw and equal-urn summaries answer different descriptive questions. To assess concentration, we rank events by \emph{original principal size} before measuring what fraction of the full signed 12-month benefit is attributable to the largest 1, 5 and 10\% of events. Month contributions use origination month. Both 12-month and clean-duration mappings are rerun with minimum draw sizes of \$1, \$100, \$1,000, \$5,000 and \$10,000. Other grids vary borrowing rate (5--120\% annually), round-trip gas (USD 0--80), conversion friction (0--1\% each way), and a purely illustrative relative ARS access-rate wedge. The full combinations are in machine-readable CSVs.

The unit changes the question. An event median weights an urn with many draws many times; selecting the first eligible draw weights each represented urn once but discards subsequent activity. The median of within-urn amount-weighted ratios also assigns one observation to each urn while allowing its own large draws to receive greater weight. None of these three summaries identifies a representative person's payoff, because control of multiple urns by one person is unverified. The event mappings also share calendar-month FX observations. Accordingly, we report descriptive differences rather than conventional independent-event standard errors.

For the same 7,105 starts, imposing 12 months yields an ARS median of 0.56\% and a \emph{TRY median of $-4.70\%$}, compared with 2.67\% and 6.59\% for all 130,742 draw starts. The selected TRY cohort is more concentrated in 2020, when its subsequent monthly path differs from later entry cohorts; size and fixed-gas exposure also differ. At 24 imposed months the selected medians reach 15.84\% and 33.74\%, but those durations were not observed and cannot be interpreted as a feasible extension of the selected vaults. The 3- and 6-month comparisons, and their exact denominators, are in \texttt{results/same\_sample\_horizon\_comparison.csv}.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\subsection{Meaning of the borrower comparisons}
The research question has a conditional answer. For the selected repaid MakerDAO spell lengths, the stipulated LCU liability and USD-held proceeds yield a negative \emph{median} net USD difference under published daily marks and the base costs. The arithmetic is not just a repetition of the FX identity: short observed exposure, an opening fee, two conversions and gas often leave too little gross currency movement to offset debt service. Nevertheless, at the same base assumptions TRY has a slightly positive principal-weighted selected-spell total. This difference between the median and the ratio of totals, together with the first-draw and urn summaries, is evidence that ``benefit'' depends on the object being described.

The 12-month figures make the scope especially clear. The full draw-event sample has positive 12-month medians for both currencies, but keeping the selected clean starts fixed produces only 0.56\% in ARS and $-4.70\%$ in TRY. This is a sample-composition contrast at a common imposed endpoint. The selected spell sample is smaller in principal and older in opening date; holding its members for a year is itself hypothetical. The observed-versus-fixed contrast also changes which months of FX history are sampled. These descriptive comparisons cannot attribute a numerical share of the difference solely to causal loan duration, but they show that a long-horizon positive event median is a weak basis for a claim about the typical observed spell.

Cost and access assumptions matter separately. At the base rate, removing gas and conversion friction leaves negative selected-spell medians; more costly cases worsen them (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:costgrid}). For ARS, a \emph{stipulated} 10\% increase in the accessible-rate wedge at exit relative to entry turns the selected median positive, while a 20\% decrease makes it strongly negative (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:arswedge}). These are algebraic sensitivity cases with unchanged underlying official observations, not an inferred history of parallel ARS rates. A fixed premium level would cancel from $E_0/E_t$ under the symmetric swap convention, whereas changing access terms may overwhelm the short-run FX movement. An actual borrower would also face market depth, token liquidity and possibly asymmetric conversion rules.

The economic implication is conditional and two-sided. Local denomination may transfer currency risk away from a borrower whose proceeds remain in USD; a lender or token issuer must still fund and value the corresponding claim and manage redemption. The dataset does not observe the contract design or counterparties that would make that transfer possible. The highly concentrated 12-month numerator further limits the relevance of a positive principal-weighted number for ordinary positions. A practical feasibility assessment must establish accessible FX and LCU liquidity, actual borrower choice, position-size-dependent costs and enforceable settlement rules before treating the accounting range as a prediction.

\subsection{Accounting boundary for issuer capital}
The borrower-side benefit does not measure issuer capital. Borrower-posted collateral is encumbered until enforcement and is not a free asset in addition to its loan claim. Let $A_t^{\rm loan}$ be performing LCU loan claims, $A_t^{\rm recovery}$ cash from completed collateral sales whose related claims have been removed, $R_t$ other unrestricted reserves excluding that recovery cash, and $Q_t^{\rm redeem}$ and $Q_t^{\rm other}$ token redemption and other obligations. \emph{All five terms are valued in USD at the same date $t$ under a specified marking convention.} A schematic equity identity is
\begin{equation}
K_t=A_t^{\rm loan}+A_t^{\rm recovery}+R_t
-Q_t^{\rm redeem}-Q_t^{\rm other}.
\label{eq:balance}
\end{equation}
A completed sale replaces the claim with recovery; $W_{i,\tau}=D_i(\tau)-R_{i,\tau}$ writes down an \emph{asset}, while token burns or settlement must separately reduce liabilities. Keeping recovery separate from $R_t$ avoids counting the same cash twice. The identity shows why a borrower's reduced USD debt is not automatically an issuer gain. We observe no LCU liabilities, unrestricted issuer reserves or auction proceeds from which to compute $K_t$; collateral and redemption dynamics require a distinct empirical study \cite{GortonZhang2023,Arner2020,BIS2023}.

\subsection{Limits of inference and implementation}
The most consequential data limitations are \emph{selection} and \emph{FX access}. Clean repaid spells are not a survival sample of all draw events, and omitted top-level calls can alter inferred debt histories. The official BCRA, ECB and OECD/FRED series do not establish rates paid at the transaction times; the earlier-date check addresses publication timing only. No verified parallel ARS history is supplied. Repeated draws share urn-level risks and calendar FX observations; no causal effect, confidence interval or independent-borrower claim follows from treating event rows as separate people. The short-spell and long-horizon rows draw on different samples and FX frequency unless explicitly controlled as in Table~\ref{tab:samesample}.

Implementation limitations are also material. The USD-held-proceeds comparator, interest, fee, gas, conversion friction and accessible-rate wedges are stipulated scenarios. Large positions may face price impact. Fixed horizons presume survival and access to a conversion market, neither of which is observed for an LCU vault. The nonrecourse collateral-sale ledger is conditional, while within-horizon collateral paths, oracle marks, auction discounts and sale times are unavailable. The terminal diagnostic cannot identify expected borrower cash flow, peg stability, reserve adequacy, governance loss absorption or enforceable redemption. These remain questions for a real protocol and its users rather than conclusions from this trace sample.

\section{Conclusion}
We asked whether a hypothetical local-currency debt mapped to MakerDAO draw sizes, dates and selected spell lengths would have yielded a positive borrower-side USD difference after explicit costs, and how robust that description is to the sample and FX marks. The contribution is to make the underlying comparison auditable: the same selected starts are revalued at observed and imposed endpoints, quote timing and cost assumptions are varied, and event, urn and principal-weighted summaries are distinguished.

Selected clean, repaid spells last a median 6.95 days and have daily-reference median net effects of $-2.86\%$ (ARS) and $-4.30\%$ (TRY) at the illustrative 20\% rate. Earlier published-date marks leave them negative. Full-sample 12-month medians of 2.67\% and 6.59\% describe imposed horizons, while the same clean starts at 12 months yield 0.56\% and $-4.70\%$; sample composition is central to that contrast. Principal-weighted results are concentrated among large draws and differ from a typical event or urn. These comparisons show when a nominal FX mechanism is outweighed by costs in the selected sample and why a long-horizon headline must be interpreted carefully. They do not demonstrate accessible gains or issuer solvency.

The next empirical priorities are transaction-time executable FX and LCU-liquidity data, fuller on-chain debt histories, actual borrower and lender selection, and observed collateral paths with auction settlements. These would permit a test of who can access each conversion and whether the conditional cash-flow branches occur in practice. A separate issuer-level study would need liability, reserve and redemption records to evaluate peg or solvency consequences.
